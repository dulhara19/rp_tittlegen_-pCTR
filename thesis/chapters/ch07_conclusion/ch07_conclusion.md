## 7.2 Accomplishment of the Research Objectives

### 7.2.1 Objective 1.7.1 — To Identify

The objective was to identify viral linguistic features (sentiment polarity, arousal, information gap structures, numerical triggers, and the T1–T7 trigger taxonomy) correlating with high view velocity, via feature engineering on Kaggle datasets. This objective was met at the design and implementation stage (Chapter 4–5): the T1–T7 taxonomy is operationalised directly in the Analyzer and Generator agents (Section 5.4–5.5), and the pCTR feature set (Section 5.7) encodes sentiment, arousal, and category signals extracted from the same public datasets. Whether these particular features correlate as strongly with engagement as hoped is a separate, harder question the pCTR model's own evaluation numbers speak to directly rather than assume: an F1 of 0.6901 and ROC-AUC of 0.7613 (Chapter 6, §6.3) indicate the identified features carry real but moderate predictive signal: enough to drive a meaningfully better-than-chance ranking of candidate titles (the 0.94 NLI acceptance rate and 0.6853 average top-ranked pCTR of Section 6.4 are downstream evidence of this), not enough to claim a strong, fully validated feature set. This objective is judged **substantially met**, with the moderate ML performance itself constituting part of the honest answer to what was identified, not a separate failure alongside it.

### 7.2.2 Objective 1.7.2 — To Analyse

The objective was to analyse the effectiveness of Few-Shot Prompting, Chain-of-Thought reasoning, and the Persona Pattern in operationalising Information Gap Theory, Prospect Theory, and Social Proof, by comparing structured-prompting output against zero-shot baseline output. This objective is met most directly by the Chapter 6 comparison between the system (which implements all three prompting techniques, Section 5.5) and Baseline B (a single zero-shot GPT-4o prompt with none of them, Section 5.12/6.6). The LLM-as-a-Judge results (§6.7) show the system's structured-prompting output significantly outperforms Baseline A on curiosity, urgency, and overall score, and significantly outperforms Baseline B specifically on relevance, without a statistically confirmed edge over Baseline B on curiosity or urgency at n = 20. This is a genuine, evidence-grounded analysis of the technique's effectiveness, not a null result, but a bounded one: structured prompting demonstrably helps relative to no prompting engineering at all (Baseline A) and relative to zero-shot prompting on the specific dimension of staying faithful to content (relevance), while its advantage over zero-shot prompting on curiosity and urgency, though present in the raw numbers, did not reach significance at this sample size. **Met**, with the honest caveat about statistical power at n = 20 carried forward rather than smoothed over.

### 7.2.3 Objective 1.7.3 — To Design and Develop

The objective was to design and develop the three-agent system (Analyzer, Generator, Critic) as a Facade-pattern REST API Wrapper with an integrated pCTR model and NLP validation layer (VADER, BERTScore, NLI). This objective is **fully met**: Chapters 4 and 5 document the complete design and a working implementation, including the eleven functional tests (Chapter 6, §6.2) confirming the pipeline behaves correctly end-to-end, and the additional frontend component (Section 5.10) built beyond the original objective's scope as a demonstration and evaluation surface. This is the objective with the least ambiguity in its evidence: the system exists, runs, and was the platform every other objective's evaluation was conducted against.

### 7.2.4 Objective 1.7.4 — To Evaluate

The objective was to evaluate system performance using quantitative metrics (pCTR, BERTScore, VADER, Self-BLEU, NLI acceptance) and qualitative human evaluation (a ≥50-participant preference survey) against Baseline A and Baseline B, including an LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation addressing RQ4. This objective is **met, with an honest power caveat on its qualitative half**. The quantitative evaluation is complete (Chapter 6, §6.3–6.4) and the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation is complete (§6.7, 60/60 evaluations, addressing RQ4 directly). The qualitative human evaluation component was descoped from its originally stated ≥50-participant design to a 15–20 participant pilot (§6.6) for time-constraint reasons logged during Sprint 6, then distributed and collected in full: n = 29 real responses, exceeding the reduced pilot target. Its results are directionally consistent with the LLM-judge findings (the system leads both baselines on raw mean curiosity and emotional impact), but none of the Wilcoxon comparisons reach significance at this sample size, and the human/LLM inter-rater reliability check came back weak and non-significant (r = −0.14, §6.6). The objective's quantitative half stands on solid, complete evidence; its qualitative half is now also fully executed, but its statistical power is limited by the pilot's reduced scale rather than the survey being incomplete. Reporting this objective as unqualifiedly met would overstate the strength of the human-evaluation evidence specifically; reporting it as only partially met would understate that every planned measurement was actually carried out. "Met, with the pilot-scale human survey's limited statistical power carried forward honestly" is the accurate description, and is preferred here over either alternative.

## 7.3 Problems Encountered During Research and Development

This section draws together the deviations already logged in place throughout `PLAN_IMPLEMENTATION.md` and Chapters 5–6, rather than restating each in full. The intent here is to characterise the shape of the difficulties encountered, not to duplicate their individual write-ups.

**Data availability shaped the ML model more than technique choice did.** Neither Kaggle source dataset used (Section 3.4) carries a real subscriber-count or CTR field, forcing a defaulted `channel_subscriber_tier` and a category-median-Views proxy for `Virality_Score` (Section 5.7, Table 5.4). This, not the choice of Random Forest over XGBoost, or any hyperparameter decision, is the most plausible root cause of the pCTR model's F1/ROC-AUC gap against its targets (Section 6.3, 6.9). The lesson generalises beyond this specific project: for a metric-optimisation system like this one, the ceiling on model quality is frequently set by what the available data can support, not by modelling effort applied after data collection is fixed.

**A genuine concurrency bug, not merely a performance one, was found and fixed during Sprint 5.** The original sequential Critic scoring produced a ~29.5 s average latency, well outside the NFR1 budget; the first parallelisation attempt using `asyncio.gather` across the three scorers introduced a real correctness bug: concurrently loading two different transformer models (`bart-large-mnli` and `roberta-large`) on separate threads corrupted `transformers`/`accelerate`'s meta-device weight materialisation, a failure mode that reproduced reliably but was not fixed by a naive per-module lock (Section 5.8, `PLAN_IMPLEMENTATION.md` §5.2). The eventual fix, eager model loading at import time plus a lock around each model's forward pass, is documented as a deliberate architectural decision rather than a quick patch, and stands as the clearest example in this project of a problem that initially presented as a performance issue turning out to require a correctness-level fix.

**The human evaluation study had to be descoped twice under a fixed timeline, and the reasoning for each cut is preserved rather than only the final number.** The original design (10 videos × 3 titles × 4 dimensions = 120 questions) was judged impractical for genuine respondent completion; a first-pass cut to 5 videos (60 questions) was judged still too long; the final 3-video, 36-question design was the one judged realistically completable in 8–10 minutes (Section 6.6). This is recorded here as a real, non-trivial project-management problem rather than glossed as a simple scope reduction: designing a human study that is both statistically meaningful and realistically completable by volunteer participants is a genuine constraint-satisfaction problem, and this project's answer, reduce scope until a completion-time budget is met and report the reduction explicitly, is offered as the honest resolution actually reached, not necessarily the only valid one.

**Baseline A required a documented compromise from its original definition.** SOT §19.3 specifies Baseline A as "ground truth from video": a real, human-written title from the actual video. Because the evaluation set (Section 6.4) consists of synthetic transcripts with no real underlying YouTube upload, no literal ground-truth title exists to draw from; `video_description` was substituted as a documented stand-in (Section 5.12, 6.6). This is a small but real gap between what the SOT specified and what synthetic evaluation data can support, and is one of the clearer examples in this project of a design document written before implementation encountering a constraint (the absence of real published videos in the evaluation set) that implementation then had to resolve pragmatically and log rather than silently work around.

## 7.4 Self-Reflection on the Research Process

Working simultaneously as the implementer of the system and the author documenting it produced one clear methodological benefit and one clear risk, both worth naming directly. The benefit: every chapter in this thesis, from Chapter 4 onward, could be and was written as an as-built account grounded in the actual working repository rather than a forward-looking design document reconciled with reality only at the end. Chapter 5's Table 5.4 and Chapter 6's Tables 6.5–6.6 exist as artefacts of this discipline, not retrofitted summaries. The risk: a single person building and evaluating their own system is structurally closer to the results than an independent evaluator would be, which is precisely why the honest, sometimes unflattering reporting of unmet targets (pCTR F1/ROC-AUC, VADER arousal, BERTScore) and of statistically inconclusive results (the human survey's non-significant Wilcoxon tests and its weak inter-rater reliability against the LLM judge) throughout Chapter 6 was treated as non-negotiable rather than optional. The credibility of a self-evaluated system rests almost entirely on whether its author reports what did not work, or did not reach significance, as carefully as what did.

The project's own timeline discipline, keeping `PLAN_IMPLEMENTATION.md`'s sprint checklists current in real time, with deviations logged at the point they occurred rather than reconstructed later, turned out to be the single practice that made the later thesis chapters possible to write accurately at all. Chapters 5 and 6 in particular would have been substantially harder to write, and more likely to silently smooth over real deviations, without that running log to draw from.

If repeating this project, the clearest process change would be starting the human evaluation survey's distribution earlier and in parallel with the automatable evaluation work (§6.3–6.4, 6.7), rather than sequencing it after, since the two do not depend on each other. In this project the survey was in fact the last piece of Chapter 6 to close out, distributed only once the automatable evaluations were already complete; running both in parallel from the start of Sprint 6 would also have left more of the fixed timeline available to recruit toward the original n = 50+ target, rather than a 29-respondent pilot that turned out directionally right but underpowered to confirm it statistically.

## 7.5 Real-World Application Possibilities

The system as built, while explicitly scoped as a research prototype (Section 1.10) rather than a production system, points toward several realistic application contexts, distinct from the productisation work Section 1.10 excludes:

- **Independent creators and educators**, the population most directly motivated in Section 1.5.1, are the most direct beneficiaries: a creator with a transcript but no marketing background could realistically use the deployed API (Section 5.9's FastAPI Facade, or the demonstration frontend of Section 5.10) as-is for title suggestions, with the NLI faithfulness gate (Section 5.6) providing a concrete, automatic safeguard against the accidental clickbait risk a less experienced creator might otherwise introduce unknowingly.
- **Marketing and content teams at small organisations**, the non-profits and educational institutions named in Section 1.5.1, could plausibly integrate the API Wrapper into an existing content workflow tool without needing to understand or maintain the underlying multi-agent pipeline, precisely because the Facade pattern (Section 4.8) was chosen to hide that complexity behind a single REST endpoint.
- **A/B testing tooling for YouTube Studio-integrated products** is a natural, though currently out-of-scope (Section 1.10), extension: the system's title candidates could feed an external A/B testing harness rather than the system needing to implement live CTR measurement itself, which Section 1.10 explicitly and deliberately excludes from this project's own claims.

None of these applications depends on the human evaluation study reaching statistical significance to be technically usable today; all three would, however, benefit from a larger-scale replication of the survey (§7.6, recommendation 1) than this thesis's pilot-scale data (n = 29) can provide, since real user preference data at adequate statistical power is the strongest available signal for whether the system's titles are actually preferred by the people it is meant to serve, as opposed to only scoring well on automated proxies.

## 7.6 Future Recommendations

Consistent with SOT §20.2 and grounded in what Chapter 6's evaluation specifically surfaced as gaps, the following are recommended as the most consequential next steps, in rough priority order:

1. **Scale up the human evaluation survey toward the original n = 50+ design.** The pilot (n = 29, Section 6.6) is directionally consistent with the LLM-judge findings but statistically underpowered: every Wilcoxon comparison returned p > 0.1. The tooling, instrument, and analysis pipeline are all already built and proven on real data; reaching the original target sample size, and ideally restoring some of the videos cut during the two descoping rounds (Section 7.3), is the most immediate and lowest-effort recommendation here, not a long-term research direction, since it requires no new design or engineering work, only more respondents.
2. **Replace VADER with a fine-tuned arousal classifier trained on title-length text**, to close the gap identified in Section 6.4/6.9 between VADER's lexicon-coverage limitations and the system's likely true emotional-engagement performance. A more accurate instrument here would clarify whether the current VADER-arousal shortfall reflects the metric or the system.
3. **Widen the pCTR model's hyperparameter search and, where possible, source real subscriber-count and CTR data** (e.g. via the YouTube Data API with creator consent, as SOT §20.1 already proposes), to close the F1/ROC-AUC gap identified in Section 6.3, which this thesis has argued is more plausibly a data-availability limitation than a technique limitation.
4. **Thumbnail integration**, extending the system to multimodal (title + thumbnail) joint optimisation, as originally scoped out in Section 1.10 and reaffirmed in SOT §20.2, the single most requested extension in the original research design, unchanged by this project's findings.
5. **Multi-language support**, localising the T1–T7 trigger taxonomy and Prompt Database per language and cultural context, addressing the English-only exclusion in Section 1.10.
6. **Real-time A/B integration with YouTube Studio**, closing the feedback loop this system currently only approximates via the pCTR proxy model, consistent with the real-world application described in Section 7.5.
7. **A domain-specific fine-tuned LLM**, trained on high-CTR YouTube titles, as a longer-term alternative to prompt-engineering GPT-4o, which SOT §20.2 already anticipates as a way to reduce the system's current dependency on a single external API provider.

## 7.7 Chapter Summary

Of the four research objectives set out in Chapter 1, one (1.7.3, system design and development) is fully met, three (1.7.1, feature identification; 1.7.2, prompting-technique analysis; 1.7.4, evaluation) are met with an honestly reported qualification: moderate ML predictive power in one case, a statistically bounded rather than sweeping advantage over the strongest baseline in another, and a pilot-scale human survey that is directionally consistent with but not a statistically significant confirmation of the automated evaluation findings in the third. This thesis has argued throughout Chapters 4–6, and reaffirms here, that reporting this evaluation profile precisely, including every gap and every non-significant result, is itself part of a design science contribution, not a weakness to be minimised. The system was built, evaluated on real (if imperfect) data including n = 29 genuine human respondents, and every deviation from its original plan and every unmet target was logged and explained at the point it occurred rather than reconciled only in retrospect. The recommendations of Section 7.6, led by scaling the human survey toward its original statistical-power target, describe a realistic and directly actionable path from the system's current state to a fuller validation of the research question posed in Chapter 1.
